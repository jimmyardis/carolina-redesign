The Conservation
Ledger

What the state has paid to conserve land, where that land is, and what it covers.

Since 2004 the South Carolina Conservation Bank has awarded money to private landowners, land trusts and state agencies to keep land out of development. This page follows those dollars onto the ground: every funded property, mapped, with the habitats it protects and the ones it doesn't. It argues for nothing. Every figure is computed from public data and every caveat is printed next to the number it qualifies.

Built from public records.

The money

Four hundred and fifty-three million dollars, and a four-year hole

Fiscal years run 1 July – 30 June and are labelled by the year they start, matching the Bank's own convention. Years with no bar had no grants at all — they are shown rather than skipped.

The gaps in the bars

Four years with no grants, and then a fifth

The Bank made no grants at all in FY2008-09 through FY2011-12, and none in FY2017-18. Those are real gaps in the record, not missing data. Two different things were going on.

FY2017-18 — documented

The Bank is funded by 25¢ of the state's $1.30 deed recording fee under S.C. Code §12-24-95. For FY2018 that transfer was switched off. The State Inspector General's review of the Bank records it plainly:

“In FY 2018, the Bank's funding from the state deed recording fee was suspended in accordance with Proviso 53.1.” SC Office of the State Inspector General, Review of the South Carolina Conservation Bank's Accounting Practices and Grant Funding Procedures, Case #2017-2554-I, January 2018.

FY2008-09 to FY2011-12 — mechanism documented, the years are not

The Conservation Bank Act carried a self-suspending clause from the start. Section 5 of Act 200 of 2002 provided that:

“In a fiscal year when the General Assembly in the annual general appropriations act provides less appropriations than what was provided for the previous year to at least one-half of the state agencies or departments contained therein the act or in any year when the Budget and Control Board orders across the board cuts to state agencies and departments in the manner provided by law, no further transfer of deed recording fees or other appropriated funds, state or local, may be credited to the trust fund for the fiscal year or balance of the fiscal year” Act 200 of 2002, §5, as quoted in S. 139 of 2011-2012, the bill that proposed deleting it.

So a mechanism existed that would have zeroed the Bank's funding in a year of across-the-board cuts, and the legislature moved to repeal it in 2011. What we could not establish is that the clause was applied in each of those four years. No source reviewed states that, and the Bank's own published annual reports only go back to 2019 and do not discuss the period. The gap in the chart is a fact; its cause is not documented in the sources reviewed here.

Awards vs. cash

What was awarded, and what actually moved

Three different things, kept apart throughout: an appropriation is what the General Assembly authorised; an award is what the Bank committed to a property; a recorded payment is money that actually moved, from the Comptroller General's FY2025 transaction files. The payment data covers one fiscal year only, so it is a cross-check, not a trend. The vendor named on a land payment is normally the closing attorney or title company, not the land trust — amounts reconcile, names do not.

Where it went

Every funded property

Each shape is one grant. Colour by what the money bought, how much it cost, or when it happened.

Boundaries are as published by the Conservation Bank. Some easement boundaries elsewhere in the record are generalised for landowner privacy, and which ones is no longer recorded anywhere we can read: the National Conservation Easement Database carried that flag, and it stopped being updated in January 2025. The Protected Areas Database that replaced it does not carry it. Easement outlines here should be read as approximate.

By county

Where the dollars and the acres diverge

The counties that received the most money are not the counties that conserved the most land. Coastal acreage is dear; upstate and midlands acreage is not.

Cost per acre is not a performance measure. Land value, habitat type, deal structure and how much the landowner donated vary enormously between deals. A cheap acre and an expensive acre are usually buying different things. Grants spanning two counties are split evenly between them, because no per-county breakdown is published.

The land

What is protected, and what isn't

Coverage is protected acres over total remaining acres of that land-cover class, computed by overlaying the deduplicated conserved estate on the 2021 National Land Cover Database at 30 m. NLCD reports land cover, not habitat type, so it cannot tell longleaf sandhill from any other evergreen forest. Priority-habitat coverage needs the State Wildlife Action Plan layers, which are not yet in hand.

The conserved estate

Who actually holds the protected land

The total on its own means very little. These categories are a partition, not a list of overlapping claims: each acre is counted once, under whoever holds it, so the rows add to the estate. Nearly half of it is federal, and most of that is two national forests. Land under a conservation easement is still privately owned and usually still farmed or logged, whether the easement is held by a land trust or by a government agency — both are shown separately from land a trust or an agency owns outright. Easements held by a federal body, mostly wetland-reserve easements on working farms, are the largest share of that public-held group.

GAP status is the protection-strength code PAD-US assigns. GAP 1 is a strict ecological reserve; GAP 4 is multiple-use land with no mandate to protect biodiversity. Only the GAP-coded sources carry it, so these tiers cover less than the full estate.

Double counting

Why you cannot add these numbers up

One tract can be a Conservation Bank grant, a state Heritage Preserve, a PAD-US easement and a row in the Nature Conservancy's compilation, all at once. Adding the sources together inflates the answer.

The deduplicated figure is a spatial union of every source, so each acre is counted once no matter how many registries list it. Summing the sources instead would inflate the answer by —.

What didn't reconcile

The unresolved column

These grants stay in every total on this page. They are in the Bank's own register, so they are real awards of real money; what is missing is an independent registry listing the land. On the map they carry the flag Bank-reported only, meaning: reported by the Conservation Bank, not found in an independent registry. Nothing in the review queue has been resolved by the pipeline — a wrong match is worse than an admitted gap, so matches below the confidence threshold are published as open questions rather than quietly decided.

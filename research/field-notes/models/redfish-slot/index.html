How the model works, and what it can't tell you

The fish

The model follows red drum from their first autumn to old age, one year at a time, using the life history in SCDNR's 2025 South Carolina assessment1. That means an age-varying growth curve toward a maximum length of 113 cm1, fish living up to 41 years1, and half of females spawning by 4.2 years1. Small fish die of natural causes more often than big ones, scaled to body length as the assessment does. As in the assessment, the number of young fish each year doesn't depend on how many spawners there are. That is a modeling choice with real uncertainty behind it.

The fishing

Anglers catch fish of all sizes, mostly juveniles in the estuaries. They keep a share of the fish inside the slot and release everything else. Released fish die at the rate you set, 8%1 by default, the assessment's assumption.

How it was fitted

Nine settings were fitted to SCDNR's own numbers for 43 seasons: the size range anglers catch most, and the share of legal fish they keep under each bag limit. For each season the model took the assessment's estimate of fishing pressure on two-year-olds and that season's rules, and was scored on the assessment's spawning potential and on the survey's share of caught fish that were kept.

The biggest assumption is the new one-fish bag. No season has been fished under it yet. Across the 5-, 3- and 2-fish years, the share kept fell as the bag shrank, and the presets extend that trend to one fish: . If you think anglers will adapt differently, move the slider.

The model also predicts a number it was not fitted to: dead released fish as a share of all fish killed. The assessment reports more than 30%1 since 2017.

Status, in the assessment's own terms

SCDNR judges overfishing on a three-season average of spawning potential. For 2021–2023 that average was 0.3031, just above the line, helped by strong year classes. The single 2023 season was 0.1371. The regional southern-stock figure that drove the new rules is 0.2071.

What it can't tell you

  • Where fish are caught, environmental effects on spawning success, and changes in angler behavior beyond the keep share.
  • Uncertainty in the recreational catch surveys. SCDNR notes pilot studies suggesting the effort survey may be overestimated up to 40%1.
  • Each season is replayed as if the fishing pattern had been in place long enough to settle. Real year classes carry the past forward.
  • Good and bad spawning years use the assessment's recruitment variability (0.371) but a fixed random sequence, not a forecast.

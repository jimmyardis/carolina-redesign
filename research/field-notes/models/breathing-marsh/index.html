The Breathing Marsh

A salt marsh at Edisto Beach, drawn at the real height of the tide as you read this. Twice a day the ocean floods in and drains away, and every plant and animal here lives by that rhythm.

Tide heights are NOAA predictions1 and can differ from actual water levels during wind and storms.

The month's rhythm

Each bar is one day's highest tide. The tides run higher around the new and full moon (spring tides), when they flood the high marsh, and lower around the half moons (neap tides). Tap a day to jump to it.

Where the numbers come from

The tide

Tide heights are NOAA's official predictions for Edisto Beach, Edisto Island (station 86676301)1, in feet above mean lower low water, the zero point on nautical charts: the average of the lower low water height of each tidal day2. NOAA's tide gauge at Edisto was removed in 19821. Its predictions are now made from Charleston (8665530)1 with time and height offsets, and NOAA publishes only the highs and lows. The page draws the water between them as a smooth half-wave.

Predictions run through . Actual water levels can differ with wind, storms and river flow. The averages below come from the 1983–20011 tidal epoch. Sea level has risen since, so today's tides run a little higher than these averages.

The zones

Each zone's height is set by the tide, not by a survey of one marsh. Smooth cordgrass grows between mean high water and mean sea level3, and creek-bank cordgrass grows nearly twice3 as much as the higher interior. The table turns each zone's tidal limits into feet at Edisto. The drawing exaggerates height so the few feet that matter are visible; a real marsh is nearly flat. The share of time each zone spends underwater is calculated from the tide predictions at the middle of its band.

The split between tall and short cordgrass, halfway between mean sea level and mean high water, is a drawing choice. The sources place tall cordgrass on the lower creek banks and short cordgrass on the higher interior, but give no single height for the change.

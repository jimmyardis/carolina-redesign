Haiti right now

The situation in October 2026

Haiti has nearly 12 million people and no elected national leader. President Jovenel Moïse was assassinated in 2021, the last senators' terms ran out in 2023, and the transitional council created in 2024 expired in February without a successor. An appointed prime minister now governs by decree. Elections set for December were postponed indefinitely on October 5.

In the vacuum, armed gangs became the strongest organized force in the country. Most belong to a coalition called Viv Ansanm ("living together"), formed in 2023 when rival groups in the capital stopped fighting each other and turned on the state. The United States designated it a terrorist organization in 2025. Gangs control most of Port-au-Prince, tax the highways at checkpoints, kidnap for ransom, and have pushed out into the farm country of the Artibonite valley and the Central Plateau. How much of the capital they hold depends on who is counting. The Associated Press now puts it at about 70%, reflecting ground security forces retook this year, mostly downtown. The UN, the Council on Foreign Relations and most outlets still cite about 90%, and people living there often put it higher. Nobody has published a surveyed map, so treat any single number as an estimate. Anyone resisting can be killed: the UN counted 241 people killed between July and early October for allegedly resisting gangs.

Against them are an under-strength national police force, a small rebuilt army, a UN-backed Gang Suppression Force at 28% of its authorized size, vigilante groups, and a private military company.

The Blackwater founder's drone war

On October 17, CNN published an inside look at the most controversial player in this fight: the Vectus Global Task Force, a private military company run by Erik Prince, who founded Blackwater. Blackwater contractors killed 17 Iraqi civilians at Nisour Square in Baghdad in 2007. Haiti's interim government hired Vectus in 2025, and since March of that year its contractors have flown explosive-laden drones in support of elite police units.

Supporters say the drones have done what nothing else could. A September UN assessment credited security operations with halting gang expansion and clearing parts of the capital, including downtown. Prince says police were losing armored trucks constantly before his team arrived and rarely lose them now. Critics answer that extrajudicial strikes in dense neighborhoods break international law, that no major gang leader has been killed in years, and that the main highway north is still blocked 18 months after Prince said key roads would reopen within a year. In early October the UN human rights chief said more than half of the roughly 5,700 people killed or injured in Haiti so far in 2026 were hurt in security operations.

Paying a private force with a share of customs revenue is a very old arrangement called tax farming, and it continues a thread you'll see in chapter 1: Haitian governments handing organized force to someone other than an accountable public institution.

Why it keeps getting worse

Haiti is usually described as a list of disasters: earthquake, assassination, gangs, hunger. A list hides the mechanism. The more useful question is why each shock made the next one more likely, and why the fixes tried so far have tended to stall. Each chapter below takes one established model, applies it to Haiti, and lets you push on its assumptions. None of the models is the whole truth. Read together, they describe a system with several reinforcing loops and only a few weak brakes.

The interactive models are teaching tools. Their parameters are set to be plausible and are labeled as illustrative; the charted figures come from the UN, IOM, World Bank and IMF sources listed at the end.

The long fuse

Path dependence · extractive institutions
The lens. Institutional economists (North; Acemoglu and Robinson) argue that early rules about who extracts from whom tend to persist, because the people who benefit from them gain the power to keep them. Critical junctures are the rare moments when the path can change.

Haiti's starting conditions were unusually hard. It won independence in 1804 through the only successful slave revolution in history, and was then made to pay for it: in 1825 France demanded an indemnity of 150 million francs as the price of recognition, later reduced to 90 million. Servicing it, and the loans taken to pay it, drained public revenue for generations. A state born owing money learned to live off customs at a few ports, controlled by a narrow circle in the capital.

Read the timeline for one pattern in particular: again and again, organized force was handed to someone other than a professional, accountable state. The Tontons Macoutes served a ruler rather than the law. The army was disbanded in 1995. Gangs became rented muscle for politicians and businesses. Filter the events by the loop they fed.

What the lens explainsWhy Haiti's elites have long had more to gain from controlling a weak state than from building a strong one, and why violence repeatedly became a private service rather than a public monopoly.
2

Roving and stationary bandits

Public choice · Mancur Olson
The lens. Olson asked why a bandit would ever behave like a government. His answer: a roving bandit who expects to move on takes everything. A bandit who expects to stay has a stake in the territory's output, so he taxes at a lower rate and may even provide order. The key variable is the time horizon.

Haitian gangs show both modes. Some act like stationary bandits, running toll checkpoints on national roads and taxing markets on a predictable schedule. Others act like roving bandits: mass kidnappings, burning neighborhoods, massacres such as Pont-Sondé in 2024. The model below shows why the horizon matters, and it has an uncomfortable implication for the current crackdown.

Slide the tenure to one season and the bandit takes everything. Stretch it out and the best rate falls, because killing the goose costs him future eggs. Now apply it to 2026. A gang suppression force that raises the odds a leader loses his turf shortens his horizon. Until pressure is strong enough to actually end his control, the model predicts more predatory behavior in the meantime, not less. UN officials told the Security Council this month that gangs are adapting their tactics as operations intensify, and that hundreds of people were killed in the West and Artibonite departments for allegedly resisting them.

What the lens explainsWhy half-strength enforcement can raise short-run violence, and why a credible exit (chapter 7) matters as much as firepower: it changes what a leader expects his future to be.

When the agents became principals

Principal–agent theory · selectorate theory
The lens. In a principal–agent relationship, someone hires someone else to act for them. The risk is that the agent gains information or power the principal can't control. Selectorate theory (Bueno de Mesquita and colleagues) adds that leaders who rely on a tiny winning coalition spend on private rewards for that coalition rather than on public goods.

For years Haitian gangs were agents. Politicians paid them to turn out votes or suppress protests; some business interests paid them to guard warehouses, block rivals or control access to ports and roads. Canada sanctioned several prominent business figures in 2022 and the United States sanctioned former president Michel Martelly in 2024, each over alleged links to armed groups. Then the agents acquired their own revenue, their own guns, and in 2023 their own federation, Viv Ansanm. In early 2024 they forced a prime minister out. Toggle the diagram to see the relationship flip.

What the lens explainsWhy sanctions on elite financiers matter even after gangs became self-funding: they cut the protection from prosecution that let the arrangement grow. It also explains why gang leaders now behave like political actors, demanding a seat at negotiations.
4

The fiscal trap and the exit valve

Fragile-state economics · Hirschman's exit, voice and loyalty
The lens. A state needs revenue to provide security, and needs security to collect revenue. Below some threshold the two pull each other down. Albert Hirschman added a second dynamic: when an institution declines, members can complain and push for change (voice) or leave (exit). When exit is easy, the people most able to exercise voice are the first to go.

Haiti collects tax equal to about 5% of GDP, one of the lowest ratios in the world. Families abroad send home roughly three times that. Remittances keep households alive and the currency stable; they also make emigration the most rational household strategy, drain the professionals who staff courts and hospitals, and give gangs something to extort.

The exit valve is now narrowing from both sides. Tens of thousands of Haitians have been forcibly returned this year, mostly from the Dominican Republic; more than 110,000 by July, according to IOM. U.S. immigration policy shifts put remittance growth at risk, and the IMF has flagged that a sharp drop in remittances is one of the main downside scenarios. Close the exit without restoring voice and the pressure has to go somewhere.

What the lens explainsWhy the economy can keep shrinking for seven years without a final collapse (the diaspora is a shock absorber), and why that absorber also quietly weakens the institutions that would end the crisis.
5

The iron pipeline

Black-market price theory · supply-side enforcement
The lens. When a good is banned in one place and legal next door, its price gap measures enforcement risk and funds the smugglers. Interdiction works on supply by raising costs; it rarely ends the trade while demand and margins stay high.

Haiti makes no guns and no ammunition. UNODC found that most trafficked firearms come from the United States, often shipped from Florida concealed among ordinary goods, and that a handgun bought for $400–500 at a licensed U.S. dealer can sell for as much as $10,000 in Haiti. Haiti has about 1,771 km of coastline and a 392 km land border, patrolled by a police force that is itself a gang target.

A twenty-fold markup means a smuggler still profits even if most loads are seized, so seizures alone raise prices more than they cut volume. The UN Security Council renewed its arms embargo and sanctions regime this month, and Haiti's deputy UN ambassador told the Council the guns will keep finding a way in. The model suggests the strongest lever sits at the source and in the money trail, not at Haiti's shoreline.

What the lens explainsWhy the gangs' firepower kept growing during years when the state was weakest, and why gun supply is the one input largely set outside Haiti.
6

The balloon

Displacement of crime · spatial diffusion
The lens. Squeeze one end of a balloon and the air moves to the other. Drug-policy and policing research calls this the balloon effect: pressure in one area pushes armed groups and their violence along roads into places that were safer. Displaced civilians follow, and their arrival strains the new place.

Step through the years. The pattern runs outward from the capital's core along the main roads: north to the Artibonite rice valley, east toward the Dominican border through Croix-des-Bouquets and Mirebalais, and up into the hills at Kenscoff. By September 2026, IOM found nearly 80% of displaced people living outside Port-au-Prince, with the sharpest recent rise in Artibonite, once a refuge.

What the lens explainsWhy concentrating force on the capital can look like success there while the toll climbs elsewhere, and why holding cleared ground with police, courts and services matters as much as clearing it.
7

The commitment problem

Game theory · Fearon on why conflicts persist
The lens. James Fearon showed that wars often continue not because sides can't imagine a deal, but because neither can trust the other to keep one once the balance of power shifts. A deal needs a guarantor whose promises will outlast the moment.

Suppose a gang leader is offered a path out: lay down arms in exchange for reduced sentences or reintegration. Should he take it? It depends on whether the promise will be kept by a government that doesn't exist yet. Haiti's interim prime minister governs by decree; the transitional council's mandate expired in February with no successor; elections planned for December were postponed indefinitely this month. Nobody now in office can bind the people who will hold office next.

Raise only the suppression odds and the deal eventually wins, but slowly and at high cost. Raise credibility and it wins much sooner. That is the case for pairing force with legal off-ramps that a successor government can't easily revoke, which is what the UN's representative urged this month with his call for disarmament and reintegration programs, especially for recruited children. The same logic runs for voters and candidates: with 759,257 people registered out of roughly 6.5 million eligible, nobody can promise an election that will be seen as legitimate, so nobody invests in one.

What the lens explainsWhy elections keep slipping (each actor waits for security, security waits for a legitimate government) and why external guarantors keep being asked to fill the gap.

The loop machine

System dynamics · feedback loops and leverage points
The lens. System dynamics, from Jay Forrester and Donella Meadows, maps how stocks (things that accumulate) change through flows governed by feedback. Reinforcing loops amplify a change; balancing loops resist it. Leverage points are places where a small push changes the system's behavior.

Here the earlier chapters join into one diagram. Every loop but one is reinforcing. The single balancing loop, outside force pushing gang power down, works with a long delay, which is exactly the kind of loop that produces oscillation and false dawns. Tap a loop to trace it.

Its equations are deliberately simple and its numbers are illustrative; the point is to see which combinations of levers break the reinforcing loops and which only reshuffle the damage. What the runs tend to show

Force alone cuts gang power the most, but killings nearly double first and the four-year toll ends up higher than the status quo. Cutting the arms supply or opening exit ramps lowers gang power more slowly while costing fewer lives, and either one compounds with pressure. Elections held while gangs are strong fail and cost legitimacy. A remittance collapse shrinks the economy, which feeds recruitment over time. The combined preset is the only run where an election succeeds. These are the model's assumptions talking, but they line up with what the UN's representative told the Security Council this week: security operations alone are insufficient, and lasting stabilization needs the state's permanent presence.

Signals to watchForce fill rate against the 5,550 ceiling. Whether cleared neighborhoods get police stations and courts, not just patrols. Voter registration against 6.5 million. Remittance volumes as U.S. policy shifts. Killings outside the West department, the clearest test of the balloon.

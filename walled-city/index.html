About twenty years ago, before the AI tools that make this kind of work easy, I copy-pasted thousands of Charleston property records into a spreadsheet by hand, one cell at a time. The question driving that work was simple but unfashionable: the dense, mixed-use, organic-grid pattern of the original American city is not just more beautiful than what came after. It also pays its bills.

Modern suburban zoning isolates uses, mandates parking, and spreads cars and asphalt across landscapes that, once paved, struggle to generate enough tax revenue to maintain the infrastructure that serves them. Joe Minicozzi's firm Urban3, working with Strong Towns, has spent two decades documenting this in cities around the country. The most economically productive land in almost any American city — measured in property-tax dollars per acre — is almost always the oldest, densest, most walkable part of town. The part that pre-dates the codes that make it illegal to build anything like it today.

This analysis tests that hypothesis directly. Two areas of equivalent size — the original 1704 walled city (67.7 acres) and a same-size stretch of Savannah Highway suburban commercial corridor in West Ashley (53.6 acres of taxable parcels inside a slightly larger polygon) — are pulled from the same county GIS, taxed at the same City of Charleston millage of 313.6, and compared parcel by parcel.

Same city. Same tax rate. The Walled City produces 12.4× more property tax per acre than its modern suburban counterpart.

Total appraised value: the walled city carries $1.31B; the suburban strip carries $72.91M. That's a ratio of about 18-to-1 on land that is roughly the same size. The walled city's 510 parcels are more than 2× the count of the strip's 177, which begins to tell the structural story: dense fine-grained parcels carry more value, more buildings, more uses, and ultimately more municipal revenue than the coarse-grained format that replaced them.

What makes the strip leak value

Look at the land-to-improvement ratio. In the walled city, the land is worth 36% of total appraised value and the buildings are worth 64% — a healthy split that says the buildings are pulling their weight. On the Savannah Highway strip, the same split is 50% land / 50% improvements. Nearly half the value is in the dirt. That's the Urban3 "Detroit problem" in early form: when the buildings stop carrying their weight, the land is paying for itself, which is structurally what happens when most of an acre is parking lot.

Look at the use mix. The walled city's 67.7 acres are mostly residential (about 70%), with the remainder a fine-grained mix of commercial, institutional, parking, and specialty uses. The Savannah Highway strip's 53.6 acres are more mixed by share — only 55% residential, 19% commercial, and a notable institutional share — but the parcels are bigger, the buildings sit further apart, and parking is the visible spine of the place. Mixed use, in this case, doesn't mean the same thing.

How the distribution tells the story

The single most informative chart in this analysis is the distribution of tax revenue per acre across each area's parcels. Plotted on a log scale, the walled city's distribution is shifted dramatically to the right of the suburban strip's — both have a long tail at the high end, but the walled city's median ($440K per acre) is roughly an order of magnitude above the suburban strip's ($22K per acre).

Who's at the top of each list

The most productive parcels per acre tell you what kind of intensity each pattern actually rewards. In the walled city, the top performers are small commercial and high-value mixed-use buildings at the urban core. On the Savannah Highway strip, the top performers are the comparatively rare parcels where the building actually does the work — the hotel, the high-revenue retail box — rather than the dealerships, drive-thrus, and surface lots that dominate by acreage.

It still works as a neighborhood

One result is worth sitting with. The walled city is not, despite three centuries of hospitality, tourism, and commercial pressure, primarily a tourist district. 222 of 510 parcels — 44% — are owner-occupied primary residences, taxed at South Carolina's 4% assessment ratio rather than the 6% rate that applies to commercial, rental, and second-home property. Roughly half of the walled city is still somebody's house.

The Savannah Highway strip's share is lower (31% owner-occupied) — not because there's no residential there, but because the residential is increasingly rentals, condos, and apartment complexes operated by holding companies. The fine-grained owner-occupied house, the bedrock of South Carolina's 4% assessment ratio, is more concentrated in the historic core than in the suburb.

The honest caveats

What this analysis is not

The walled city polygon (67.7 acres) is slightly larger than the historic 62-acre footprint because no public GIS layer exists for the 1704 wall; we constructed the polygon from four documented bastion corners. Boundary precision is about ±1 city block. The Savannah Highway polygon is a representative chunk of suburban commercial corridor in West Ashley, not a precise "average" of suburban Charleston — pick a different chunk and the numbers move modestly, but the order-of-magnitude conclusion does not. Tax estimates assume 313.6 mills and the standard 4%/6% SC assessment ratio; historic-preservation easement reductions and similar credits would lower the walled-city figure modestly. The walled city would still out-earn the strip by a wide margin.

Methodology, briefly

The walled city polygon is anchored to four documented bastion corners: Granville's Bastion (under the Missroon House, 40 East Bay Street), Craven's Bastion (under the U.S. Custom House steps, 200 East Bay), Carteret Bastion in the northwest (anchored to the 1713 Powder Magazine at 79 Cumberland Street, which sits twenty yards south of the north wall), and Ashley/Colleton Bastion in the southwest at Meeting and Water Streets. Boundary authority: Butler (2022) "Creating a Walled City: The Charleston Enceinte of 1704" (Charleston Time Machine, CCPL) and HALS SC-24 (Library of Congress).

The Savannah Highway comparison polygon is a roughly 69-acre rectangle along the commercial corridor in West Ashley, from approximately 32.7870°N to 32.7915°N and -80.0155°W to -80.0095°W, chosen because it captures a coherent stretch of suburban auto-oriented retail (the Hendrick Automotive group of dealerships, hotels, strip retail, the Mulberry Place Apartments) plus adjacent residential — a representative chunk of the City of Charleston's West Ashley side, governed by Tax District 63 at the same combined millage (313.6) and same 4% / 6% SC assessment ratios as the peninsula.

Parcels for both areas were pulled in a single spatial-intersect query against Charleston County's CDC_ParcelMap ArcGIS REST service. We retained only parcels whose centroid falls inside each polygon. Every parcel carries the County's official Appraised Value, Land Appraised Value, Improvement Appraised Value, Class Code, Legal Residence flag, owner name, tax district, and most-recent sale price and date.

Property tax is estimated parcel-by-parcel as Appraised × Ratio × 313.6 ÷ 1000 − LOST credit, where Ratio is 4% for Legal Residence = Y parcels and 6% otherwise. The LOST (Local Option Sales Tax) credit applies as 0.00090 × the taxable amount. This estimate ignores any historic-preservation easement reductions, conservation-easement reductions, and other parcel-level adjustments that may apply.

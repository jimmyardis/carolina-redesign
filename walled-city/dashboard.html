A side-by-side property analysis · Charleston, South Carolina

The walled city vs. the suburban strip.

Two patches of Charleston. Same city. Same tax rate. Two very different per-acre realities. The 1704 enceinte and a same-size chunk of Savannah Highway in West Ashley, both inside the City of Charleston, both taxed at Tax District 313.6 mills — and the older one out-earns its modern counterpart by a wide margin.

Why this matters

For 100 years, American cities have been building suburban patterns under the assumption that lower-density, separated-use, parking-mandated development is the "responsible" way to grow. The framework Chuck Marohn and Joe Minicozzi popularized — Strong Towns and Urban3 — flipped that on its head: when you measure property-tax productivity per acre of land, the oldest, densest, most walkable parts of almost every American city out-earn the newer suburban districts by an order of magnitude or more.

Charleston's walled city — the original 1704 enceinte, built for military defense and persisting as the architectural and economic spine of the city ever since — is one of the cleanest natural experiments in the country for this claim. Same city. Same tax structure. A roughly comparable acreage of modern suburban Charleston, when held up next to it, produces a fraction of the per-acre revenue. The difference isn't aesthetics. It's fiscal arithmetic.

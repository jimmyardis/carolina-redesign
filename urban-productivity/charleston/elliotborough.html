Charleston · Cannonborough-Elliotborough · A vignette

Elliotborough pays its way.

A 130-acre slice of Cannonborough-Elliotborough — the eastern half, bounded by Meeting Street, Rutledge Avenue, Septima Clark Parkway, and Bee Street — generates an estimated $19.5 million in annual property tax for the City of Charleston. That's $149K per acre per year, on a footprint smaller than two walled cities. The neighborhood pays substantially more per acre than the full Cannonborough-Elliotborough average, and yet is treated, in the words of one local developer, "like a red-headed stepchild" relative to neighborhoods south of Calhoun Street.

By Jimmy Ardis · Carolina Redesign · A focused companion piece to The Walled City Still Pays.

The framing for this analysis is specific: the most economically valuable part of Cannonborough-Elliotborough is Elliotborough — the eastern half, bounded by Meeting Street, Rutledge Avenue, Septima Clark Parkway, and Bee/Morris/Mary Streets. The full CENA polygon (Septima Clark to the north, President Street to the west) is roughly 200 acres. Elliotborough is about 137 acres of that. The methodology this report applies to the walled city — parcel-level property tax productivity per acre, computed against the same City of Charleston combined millage of 313.6 and the same SC 4%/6% assessment ratios — applies just as cleanly here.

The headline: $149K per polygon acre. That's 16% more productive per acre than the full CENA average ($129K/ac), confirming the prevailing sentiment that the eastern half is doing the heavier fiscal lifting. It's less than half the walled city's $334K/ac — the historic core is unique — but it puts Elliotborough comfortably in the top tier of Charleston neighborhoods on a per-acre basis. 576 parcels carry $1,078 million in appraised value and pay an estimated $19.5 million per year in property tax.

For 130 acres of working neighborhood — most of it residential, with a commercial spine along Upper King Street — that's not a "red-headed stepchild." That's a major contributor to the city's tax base.

Three layers of pre-zoning Charleston, three productivity tiers. The walled city is the historic peak. Elliotborough, just north of Calhoun Street, captures the next tier — borough-era density and use mix, with a commercial spine that pulls its weight. The fuller CENA polygon dilutes that productivity by adding the western half (Cannonborough proper, bounded by Rutledge to the east and President to the west), which is less commercially dense.

The King Street spine

Elliotborough's most productive parcels cluster along Upper King Street, where the neighborhood's commercial activity concentrates. Of the top ten parcels by tax-per-acre, eight are addressed on King Street between Calhoun and Spring. The two non-King-Street leaders are at 28 Woolfe Street (recent development on a tight footprint) and 45 Cannon Street (a small but high-tax parcel).

The top three parcels alone — 28 Woolfe, 595 King, and 484 King — represent $130 million in appraised value on 1.4 combined acres. That's the kind of fine-grained, building-intensive development the walled city built three centuries ago, replicated here in modern form. When developers talk about the productivity Elliotborough generates, this corridor is what they mean.

The structural finding worth flagging

Elliotborough's land share of appraised value is 48% — meaningfully higher than the walled city's 35%. That means roughly half the property value in the neighborhood is in the land itself, not in what's been built on top of it. Urban3 calls this the "Detroit problem" indicator: when the buildings stop carrying their weight on the dirt they sit on, the neighborhood is structurally underdeveloped.

For Elliotborough, this is good news, not bad. It means there's substantial room for incremental redevelopment — building more on the existing lots, adding accessory structures, replacing single-story commercial with two-story mixed-use — without disrupting the existing fabric. The land is signaling that it's ready for more.

Only 19% of parcels are owner-occupied primary residences. That's much lower than the walled city's 28% and reflects the high share of rentals, condos, and commercial use in Elliotborough — fine-grained, but with absentee ownership patterns that argue for active public-realm investment from the city.

What this could mean for policy

The case for public-realm investment

Elliotborough generates about $19.5 million per year in property tax for the City of Charleston — roughly equal to what Mt. Pleasant pays the city for fire-district services annually, on a footprint less than 1% the size. In addition, Upper King Street produces substantial hospitality, sales, and tourism-related revenue that isn't captured in this property-tax analysis.

The neighborhood has not received public-realm investment at the level of its fiscal contribution. The recommendation that local developers have been advancing for years is straightforward: reinvest a meaningful share of the revenue that Elliotborough generates back into the neighborhood. Traffic calming, tree planting, sidewalk improvements, streetlight upgrades, public-art commissions. The cost of those interventions is modest relative to what the neighborhood pays the city. The benefit — a safer, more pedestrian-friendly, more economically resilient corridor — would compound for decades.

The per-acre productivity math gives the argument concrete numbers to attach to it. If the city allocated even 10% of Elliotborough's annual property tax revenue back to neighborhood public-realm improvements, that's $2.0 million per year for traffic calming and tree planting in 130 acres. Compared to the cost of similar investments south of Calhoun, that scale of reinvestment would dramatically reshape the neighborhood's public realm without changing a single zoning rule.

Methodology

The Elliotborough polygon is bounded by Meeting Street (east), Rutledge Avenue (west), Septima Clark Parkway (north), and Bee/Morris/Mary Streets (south). The total polygon area computes to 130.5 acres (community estimates cite approximately 137 acres; the difference is a function of where exactly the western boundary line is drawn relative to Rutledge Avenue's centerline). Parcels were derived from the Cannonborough-Elliotborough parcel set already extracted from the Charleston County CDC_ParcelMap, filtered by centroid-inside-polygon.

Tax math is identical to the rest of the analysis: parcel-by-parcel application of the SC 4% (owner-occupied) or 6% (other) assessment ratio against the City of Charleston combined millage of 313.6, net of the 0.00090 LOST credit. Civic and tax-exempt parcels (class codes 670–720: government, schools, religious, museums) are flagged and treated as paying zero property tax even when they carry market value.
